Blackbody radiation

Summary card

A blackbody absorbs all radiation that falls on it, so the radiation it gives off depends only on its temperature. Classical physics predicted it should give off infinite energy at short wavelengths. In 1900 Planck fixed this by assuming energy comes in packets, E = hf. That was the first idea of quantum physics.

Remember
  1. Blackbody = absorbs all radiation falling on it. "Black" is not its colour: hot, it glows. The Sun is nearly one.
  2. Only temperature matters: one curve per temperature. Hotter → the peak moves to shorter wavelengths.
  3. Classical failure: heating a body makes its electrons jiggle, and jiggling electrons give off radiation. Classical physics said they can give off any wavelength, and every possible wave gets the same share of the heat. There is no limit to how short a wave can be, so the total comes out infinite: the ultraviolet catastrophe.
  4. Planck's fix: radiation carries energy only in whole packets, and shorter waves need bigger packets. The jiggling electrons rarely have enough energy for a big one, so short waves get almost nothing.
  5. Classical physics was right for long waves (small packets) and wrong for short ones (big packets).

1What is a blackbody?

Everything gives off radiation. Any object warmer than absolute zero (0 K, or −273 °C) gives off electromagnetic radiation all the time. Its atoms and electrons jiggle, and jiggling electric charges send out electromagnetic waves. Radio waves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet and X-rays are all the same kind of wave, differing only in wavelength. Radiation given off this way, because something is warm, is called thermal radiation. A warm object gives off mostly infrared, which we cannot see but feel as heat. Only very hot objects give off enough visible light to glow.

Absorbing. When radiation arrives at a surface, it can bounce off, pass through, or be absorbed (turned into heat).

A blackbody absorbs all radiation that falls on it, at every wavelength, and reflects none. "Black" describes this, not its colour. A cold blackbody looks black, because nothing bounces off it. A hot one glows: everything coming from it is radiation it gives off itself. A body that is good at absorbing radiation is also good at giving it off, so at any temperature nothing gives off more radiation than a blackbody.

Why physicists use it. An ordinary object sends out a mix of reflected radiation and its own, and its own depends on what it is made of. A blackbody's radiation depends only on its temperature, so there is one universal curve for each temperature. That makes it a clean test for any theory of radiation.

How to make one: a closed box with a small hole. Radiation that enters the hole bounces around inside and is absorbed, bit by bit, long before it can find the hole again. So the hole absorbs practically everything: the hole is the blackbody. Heat the box and the walls give off radiation, which fills the box; what leaks out of the hole is pure blackbody radiation.

The Sun is very nearly a blackbody. It works like a giant box with a hole. Radiation made inside is absorbed and given off again countless times on its way out, taking thousands to hundreds of thousands of years. By the time it escapes from the 5800 K surface, it follows the blackbody curve closely, apart from some narrow dark lines where gases in the Sun's outer layer absorb particular wavelengths.

2Where classical physics fails

What experiments measured

By the late 1800s, experiments had measured how much radiation a blackbody gives off at each wavelength. The solid curve shows this. It rises, reaches a peak, and falls back to zero at short wavelengths (left). A hotter body gives off more at every wavelength, and its peak moves to shorter wavelengths.

What classical physics predicted, step by step

  1. A body is made of atoms, and atoms contain electrons: tiny particles with electric charge.
  2. Heating a body makes its atoms jiggle, and the electrons in them jiggle too. The heat energy goes into this jiggling. The hotter the body, the harder they jiggle.
  3. A jiggling electron gives off radiation. The faster it jiggles back and forth, the shorter the wave it sends out.
  4. Classical physics: the electrons can jiggle at any speed. So the body can give off radiation at any wavelength, and there is always a shorter wave possible. There are infinitely many possible waves, and far more short ones than long ones.
  5. Classical rule: heat energy spreads out evenly. Picture people passing coins to each other at random for a long time: in the end, on average, each person holds about the same amount. Classical physics says heat energy behaves the same way (this rule worked very well for gases). So every possible wave, short or long, gets the same share of the body's heat energy.
  6. Add it up. Infinitely many possible waves, each with the same share = infinite energy, almost all of it in the short waves.

That is the dashed curve. It agrees with the measurements at long wavelengths, but at short wavelengths it shoots up without limit. Even a warm stove would blast out ultraviolet and X-rays. This failure was called the ultraviolet catastrophe. Nothing was wrong with the arithmetic: something in classical physics itself had to give.

3How Planck fixed it: energy comes in packets

In 1900, Max Planck changed one thing. A jiggling electron cannot give off just any amount of energy as radiation. A wave of frequency f (how many times per second it vibrates) can only carry energy away in whole packets, each of size

E = hf h is a fixed, very small number (Planck's constant). Faster jiggling → higher frequency → shorter wave → bigger packet.

So a short wave can only be sent out if the body can put a whole, big packet into it at once. Half a packet is not allowed.

A toy example

Imagine a body that has 6 units of energy (eV, a tiny unit of energy) to give off, and only three possible waves:

  • a short wave, whose packet is 10 eV,
  • a medium wave, whose packet is 5 eV,
  • a long wave, whose packet is 1 eV.

Classical physics shares the 6 eV evenly: 2 eV to each wave.

With packets, each wave can only carry whole packets of its own size. The short wave needs 10 eV for a single packet, but there are only 6 eV, so it gets nothing. Only two ways of using the 6 eV are possible:

  • one 5 eV packet in the medium wave + one 1 eV packet in the long wave, or
  • six 1 eV packets in the long wave.

Taking both ways as equally likely, the average energy each wave carries is:

Wave (packet size)Classical: equal shareWith packets: average
Short (10 eV)2 eV0 eV
Medium (5 eV)2 eV2.5 eV
Long (1 eV)2 eV3.5 eV

The short wave is starved: its packet is bigger than all the energy available. That is the whole fix in miniature.

The same thing in a real body

  1. The jiggling electrons have a limited amount of energy, set by the temperature of the body.
  2. Long waves need only small packets, so the body sends them out easily, just as classical physics said. That is why the classical curve matches the measurements at long wavelengths.
  3. Short waves need packets bigger than the energy the jiggling electrons usually have, like the 10 eV wave in the toy example. So they are almost never sent out.
  4. So the curve falls back to zero at short wavelengths instead of shooting up, and the total energy is finite.
  5. Heat the body and its electrons jiggle harder, so they can now fill bigger packets, and the peak moves to shorter wavelengths. At room temperature even a packet of visible light is too big, which is why everyday objects do not glow.

With this one change, Planck's formula matches the measured curve at every wavelength and temperature: it is the solid curve in the graph above.

What came next. Planck thought of the packets as a rule about how jiggling electrons give off energy. In 1905 Einstein went further: radiation itself comes in packets, later called photons, each of energy hf. That explained the photoelectric effect: below a certain frequency, light knocks no electrons out of a metal however bright it is, because no single packet is big enough. In 1913 Bohr applied the same idea to atoms: electrons sit only in certain orbits, so atoms give off only certain wavelengths. Energy coming in fixed amounts, or quantisation, became the core of quantum physics.
