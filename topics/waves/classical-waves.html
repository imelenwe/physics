Classical waves

Summary card

A wave is a shape that travels. Each point along it simply repeats what the source did, a little later. The delay is the distance from the source divided by the wave's speed.

Why write a wave with complex numbers? Knowing only how far a point is from its resting position is not enough. A point passing through the middle could be on its way up or on its way down, and that one number cannot tell you which. A complex number stores two numbers together: how far the point is from rest, and how fast it is moving. The first is what you actually see; the second tells you which way it is heading.

Remember
  1. A wave is a delayed copy of its source. A point farther away does the same thing, later. Delay = distance ÷ speed.
  2. As a formula: y = A cos(ωt − kx). A is the biggest swing, ω (written ν in the book you read) sets how fast each point swings up and down, and k sets how close together the crests are.
  3. One number is not enough. "How far from rest" cannot tell "going up" from "going down", or a big wave part-way up from a small wave at its top.
  4. The complex form keeps both pieces: how far the point is from rest (the real part, what you see) and how fast it is moving (the imaginary part).
  5. Waves add. Two waves in step make a bigger wave; half a wavelength out of step, they cancel.

1A wave is a delayed copy

A wave is a disturbance that travels, while the stuff it travels through stays put: shake one end of a rope and the bump runs along, but each bit of rope only moves up and down. Its displacement y is how far it is from rest. The source at x = 0 moves up and down. The wave carries that motion along at speed v. A point a distance x away therefore does exactly what the source did, but later, by the time the wave needs to get there:

delay = distance ÷ speed = x / v so   y(x, t) = y(0, t − x/v):  "here, now" = "at the source, x/v seconds ago"

Here f = 1 Hz and v = 2 m/s, so λ = v/f = 2 m. The pink point is 1 m away, so its delay is 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5 s, which is half a period. That is why it is always doing the opposite of the source. A point 2 m away (one whole wavelength, delay one full period) moves in step with the source.

Which way does it go? With t − x/v, a crest that leaves x = 0 at t = 0 reaches x = 4 m at t = 2 s: it moves toward +x. Swap to t + x/v and the same crest moves toward −x.

2Writing it down: from cos(2πft) to cos(ωt − kx)

  1. The source. Something that repeats every T seconds is described by cos, which repeats every 2π: y(0, t) = A cos(2πf t). Check: after one period, 2πf·T = 2π, a full cycle.
  2. Add the delay. Replace t by t − x/v: y(x, t) = A cos(2πf (t − x/v)).
  3. Open the bracket. 2πf t − (2πf/v) x. Since v = λf, the factor 2πf/v is just 2π/λ.
  4. Name the two rates. ω = 2πf (how fast the angle turns per second) and k = 2π/λ (how much it turns per metre). So:
y(x, t) = A cos(ωt − kx)  → moves toward +x A cos(ωt + kx) moves toward −x

About ω. In the book you read, this is written ν (nu); we use ω because most sources do. k is called the wavenumber.

3Why write a wave as a complex number?

The problem: one number is not enough

Watch the source, y = cos(2πt) with T = 1 s. At t = 0.25 s and at t = 0.75 s its displacement is the same: y = 0. But at 0.25 s it is on its way down, and at 0.75 s on its way up. The displacement alone cannot tell these apart, because every height happens twice in each cycle. To know what a point will do next, you need its displacement and its velocity, just as with a swinging pendulum.

There is a second problem. A displacement of 0.5 could be a big wave (biggest swing 1) part-way up, or a small wave (biggest swing 0.5) right at its top. One number cannot tell you both the size of the swing and where in the swing the point is.

The fix: carry both, as one complex number

A complex number has two separate parts, a real part and an imaginary part (the part multiplied by i). The i keeps them on separate axes, so they never mix. Put the displacement in the real part and the velocity in the imaginary part:

z = A e−i(ωt − kx) = A cos(ωt − kx) − i A sin(ωt − kx)
  1. Real part = A cos(ωt − kx) = the displacement y, the thing you actually see.
  2. Velocity = how fast y changes. cos changes at the rate −sin, and the angle grows at ω per second, so velocity = −Aω sin(ωt − kx).
  3. Imaginary part = −A sin(ωt − kx) = velocity ÷ ω. Dividing by ω just makes both parts metres.
z = displacement + i · velocity ÷ ω

As time passes, z goes round a circle of radius A. Its height is the displacement; its sideways position says which way the point is moving. Press the two buttons: same height, opposite sides of the circle.

Two meanings of "direction". The complex number tells you which way a point is moving right now (up or down). Which way the wave travels (+x or −x) is set by the sign in ωt ∓ kx (section 1).

Why quantum physics needs this. For a classical rope, the complex form is a convenient bookkeeping trick: the real part is the rope. In quantum physics the wave (the wavefunction) is complex; there is no "take the real part" step. One reason: |z|² = A², the same everywhere, while cos² keeps hitting zero. A free particle must be equally likely to be found anywhere, so only the complex wave works.

4Superposition: waves add

When two waves pass through the same place, the displacements simply add: y = y₁ + y₂. That one rule is superposition. What you get depends on how far one wave is shifted relative to the other. Slide it:

Shifted by a whole wavelength (or not at all), crest meets crest and the waves build up to 2A. Shifted by half a wavelength, crest meets trough and they cancel. Anything in between gives something in between: the combined height is 2A × |cos(π × shift ÷ λ)|.

Interference (double slit)

Light from two slits reaches a point on a screen by two paths. Only the difference in path length, δ, matters:

Bright where δ = 0, λ, 2λ, …  Dark where δ = λ/2, 3λ/2, …

No energy is lost: light missing from the dark stripes turns up in the bright ones.

Standing waves (a tied string)

Two equal waves running opposite ways add to 2A sin(kx) sin(ωt): a shape that wobbles in place.

The tied ends must never move, so sin(kL) = 0, so kL = nπ, so λ = 2L/n (n = 1, 2, 3, …).

Only certain waves fit. This is the seed of quantisation.

5Check yourself

A point is 3 m from the source and the wave speed is 1.5 m/s. What is it doing now?

Exactly what the source did 3 ÷ 1.5 = 2 seconds ago.

Why isn't y = A cos(ωt − kx) enough to describe the state of a point?

Every displacement occurs twice per cycle, once on the way up and once on the way down. You also need the velocity, which the complex form carries in its imaginary part.

What is the imaginary part of z, physically?

The velocity of the point divided by ω, so that it is in metres like the displacement.

Two equal waves are shifted by half a wavelength. What is their sum?

Zero everywhere: each crest meets a trough.

Why does a string tied at both ends allow only certain wavelengths?

The ends must stay still at every instant, which forces sin(kL) = 0. Only waves that fit a whole number of half-wavelengths into the string survive: λ = 2L/n.
